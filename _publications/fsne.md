excerpt: 'The fraction of massive stars that collapse directly to BHs without a luminous explosion -- a failed SN -- is a key unknown shaping BH formation, the chemical evolution of galaxies, and gravitational-wave progenitor physics, and despite dozens of nearby core-collapse SNe, there are only two failed SN candidates. In this paper, I led the most recent search for failed SNe with the Large Binocular Telescope (LBT), a survey of all luminous stars in 27 nearby galaxies dedicated to finding failed SNe. Using 11 yr of data, I measured the failed SN fraction -- that is, the fraction of massive stars that die as failed SN -- to be $f = 0.16^{+0.23}_{-0.12}$.  In other words, roughly one in six core-collapses forms a BH without a visible explosion. This is the most precise empirical measurement to date, and the only way to improve this measurement is to find more failed SNe  My work also produced a new candidate, M101-OC1, which was a blue supergiant that vanished in 2014 and, as LBT and HST follow-up later revealed,reappeared in 2023. Though not a true failed SN, M101-OC1 and other massive stars ``behaving badly'' found in the survey demonstrate that disentangling true failed SNe from long-duration stellar variability is essential to any future search. Abstract: We present updated results of the Large Binocular Telescope Search for Failed Supernovae. This search monitors luminous stars in 27 nearby galaxies with a current baseline of 11 yr of data. We re-discover the failed supernova (SN) candidate N6946-BH1 as well as a new candidate, M101-OC1. M101-OC1 is a blue supergiant that rapidly disappears in optical wavelengths with no evidence for significant obscuration by warm dust. While we consider other options, a good explanation for the fading of M101-OC1 is a failed SN, but follow-up observations are needed to confirm this. Assuming only one clearly detected failed SN, we find a failed SN fraction f = 0.16^{+0.23}_{-0.12} at 90 per cent confidence. We also report on a collection of stars that show slow (~decade), large amplitude (ΔL/L > 3) luminosity changes.
